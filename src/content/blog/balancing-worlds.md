I know that as working parents, it is hard to balance professional and personal lives, but it is even more difficult as a special needs child’s parent. Let me tell you how I balance raising a neurotypical and a  neurodivergent child. I am not only a special needs mom but also a teaching assistant for children with special needsPrior to this I was a  stay-at-home mother for 10 years. After my second son was diagnosed as autistic, like many parents, I was overwhelmed and felt completely lost. Eventually, I started to learn more about autism and started a life-long learning journey.

After moving to the UK, I was ready to kick-start my career by helping children with special needs. Even though I hold a postgraduate degree in commerce, my personal experience with my son has given me an intensive, firsthand understanding of fine and gross motor skill development, sensory diets, and behavioral management outside the classroom environment. I realised how vital therapy is to a family’s daily life and independence.

The first thing I believe is that choosing a career you truly love is the key to balancing your personal and professional lives. Most of my friends told me that I would feel exhausted caring for special needs at home and at school.However I have never felt worn-out in my three years of working as a teaching assistant in a special school. I have enjoyed every single moment and developed my skills by learning from experiences in both places.

The next thing I believe in is following a routine. I know we all feel jaded doing the same things every day, but what I do to avoid this is to routinely change our family’s entertainment and the places we visit much like how we change our meal recipes or switch things up every season.. Most importantly, I never skip making time in my routine for my elder son, my younger son, family time, and most importantly "me time."This keeps me fresh and rejuvenated regularly.

Finally, the paramount thing I believe for success is spending time with our special children.  You will definitely see a world of difference when you start spending just 10 to 15 precious minutes with them. You will naturally want to increase that time once you see your child starting to interact with you.

The crucial secret here is to play and interact with them on *their* terms, doing what *they* like, not what you like. Think about it: when you were a child, would you have preferred to play a game of your own choice or one your elders chose for you? It is the same with our special children. Entering their world, spending time with them and playing the way they enjoy is the key.
